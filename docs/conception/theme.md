**Theme**

**Intro**

Shown on the opening screen

**The year is 1850**

**The British Empire sends its finest expeditions into the Arctic in search of the Northwest Passage. You are among the sailors of the Royal Navy — disciplined, weary, bound to duty. You will endure cold that bites like iron, nights without end, and seas where silence breathes. To survive, you must cling to each other.**

**Yet beneath the ice lies a prison older than the Flood: the abyss which harbors Abaddon, the angel of the apocalypse.** **Many ancient myths speak of him by different names, yet all proclaim a chained deity, unmaker of creation. His acolytes walk among you. They believe mankind is a plague upon the earth and that only by feeding him souls will the world be cleansed. If they succeed, the Destroyer will rise and bring the age of humanity to an end.**

**Tone**

Thematic directives for designed only

**The Mental Economy**

The contest is measured not in Sanity but in Hope. Its loss is Despair.

Hope springs from shared meals, firelight, prayer, obedience to command, and the bonds of comradeship. These are fragile, yet they make storms endurable and disasters survivable. At high Hope, even Abaddon’s shadow is met with courage. At middling Hope, resilience remains but frays, and the tone resembles the stark dignity of *The Terror* — fascinating, admirable, but no longer uplifting. At low Hope, silence suffocates and every gesture tastes of futility.

Most of the time, Northwest is told through uplift: not modern optimism, but the archaic strength of duty and service. Even in failure, this resilience commands respect.

**Conflict of Banners**

This is not good versus evil, but a clash of flawed banners. The acolytes denounce conquest, industry, war, and hypocrisy; their charges are real, but their solution is annihilation. The crew defend monarchy and empire — ideals archaic, sometimes cruel, yet for them genuine sources of unity and hope. **Players argue over survival while the lore itself questions whether survival is justified.**

Thus players navigate suspicion under the weight of two imperfect causes. Trust feels harder because neither side is pure. And yet, amid cold and judgment, loyalty, camaraderie, and discipline kindle a fire that refuses to die.

**Result**

Northwest becomes a survival-deduction game framed by biblical apocalypse but told through the shifting light of Hope. The Arctic is merciless, Abaddon inevitable, yet the crew’s archaic ideals still inspire. The feel is not cynical tragedy, but resilient ambiguity: men bound to Empire and God stand defiant against the abyss, and only at the edge of Despair does the tale fall silent.

**The Arctic Abyss**

The abyss beneath the Arctic is not the deepest of the oceans, but it is the most inaccessible. Sealed under black ice, it lies beyond the reach of man and light alike. No creature stirs there, no current warms it. It is a vault of silence and pressure, lifeless and untouched, as though the world itself conspires to keep it hidden.

In that place of perfect inaccessibility, Abaddon is harbored until the appointed time. The abyss is not only a prison but a wound in creation, locked and sealed, where the Destroyer waits unseen beneath the frozen seas.

**Acolytes’ hatred for humanity**

Moral scandal and ecological angst

**Moral Scandal**

- **Colonial Atrocity and Imperial Hypocrisy**
  - 1850 is the height of the British Empire: conquest, exploitation, and slavery’s recent abolition but lingering scars.
  - For acolytes, this proves that human civilization thrives only on domination and cruelty.
  - *“We preach civilization, but we reap only blood and chains.”*
- **Industrial Exploitation and Dehumanization**
  - Factories, child labor, tenements — the “progress” of industry already looked hellish to many critics.
  - For acolytes: if this is humanity’s future, it deserves erasure.
  - *“Men are fed to the machine like coal, their souls ground into smoke.”*
- **Religious Corruption and Hypocrisy**
  - The period teemed with sects, but also scandals: clergy enriching themselves, churches defending slavery, harsh moral codes crushing the poor.
  - For acolytes: this shows religion itself is a lie, a mask for vice.
  - *“Priests preach heaven, but dine with devils.”*
- **War and Bloodshed as Civilization’s Currency**
  - Napoleonic wars still in memory, Crimean War looming. Europe was defined by endless conflict.
  - For acolytes: humanity never rises above killing itself.
  - *“We are not builders, but butchers with banners.”*

**Practical Flavor for Acolyte Roleplay**

- *“The ice is purer than the cities we left behind. The abyss is cleaner than the hearts of men.”*
- *“We have multiplied like vermin. Nature will scour us away — Abaddon is her hand.”*
- *“Every tree we fell, every coal we burn, is proof: we are unfit to endure.”*
- *“Hope is a lie men tell themselves while they gnaw the earth hollow. The abyss tells only truth.”*

**Ecological Angst**

- **Malthusianism (1798 onward)**
  - Thomas Malthus argued that population grows faster than food supply, leading inevitably to famine, disease, and collapse.
  - By 1850 this idea was mainstream in elite circles, influencing debates on poverty, colonial policy, and even Darwin.
  - For your acolytes: Malthusian despair can become a justification for annihilation — *“We are too many, and nature will cull us anyway. Better to hasten it.”*
- **Romanticism & the Sublime**
  - Many Romantics viewed wild nature as more beautiful, pure, and sacred than industrial civilization.
  - Painters like Caspar David Friedrich or poets like Wordsworth conveyed both awe and dread at nature’s immensity.
  - For acolytes: the Arctic wilderness and its “Destroyer beneath the ice” can embody this sublime — terrifying, but also purer than human corruption.
- **Millenarian pessimism**
  - Even outside industry, many revivalist or radical sects in the 19th century believed the world was ending soon.
  - Disease, famine, and moral decay were seen as *proof* of humanity’s corruption.
  - For acolytes: worshiping Abaddon fits right into this logic — *the end is inevitable, we only prepare the way.*
